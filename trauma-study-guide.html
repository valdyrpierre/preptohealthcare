Section 1

Trauma

Unintentional injury is the #1 killer before age 45. Primary survey, resuscitation, and the complications that follow.

12 flashcards · 5 jeopardy Qs
Want to jump straight to practice?

🔎 Why Trauma Matters

  • Unintentional injury is the #1 cause of death before age 45 — more than heart disease, cancer, suicide, and homicide combined at that age range.
  • Motor vehicle crashes and falls are the leading mechanisms overall; gunshot and stab wounds peak at ages 25–44.
  • Alcohol is a major factor: ~32% of MVC fatalities, 28–43% of violent injuries, and 47% of homicides involve alcohol.

💛 Clinical Pearl — The Golden Hour

Coined by R. Adams Cowley (Maryland Shock Trauma): the first 60 minutes after injury are the most critical for survival. Concept: get the right patient to the right facility for the right intervention as fast as possible. Maryland built the first statewide EMS system in the 1970s (scene triage + air transport).

⚠ Exam Trap — Primary vs. Secondary Survey

Primary survey = ABCDE, life-threats first. Secondary survey = FAST exam, collateral history (EMS/witnesses), non-life-threatening injuries, comorbidities, family updates.

If the patient destabilizes at ANY point — go back to the primary survey. This is a classic NCLEX "what do you do next" trap.

Fluid Resuscitation & Mass Transfusion

Core Actions

  • Two large-bore IVs (may need a central line); warmed IV fluids/blood.
  • Balanced crystalloid (LR) via pressure bag or rapid infuser, up to 1 L/min.
  • Mass transfusion protocol: PRBCs : Platelets : FFP in a 1:1:1 ratio.
  • O-negative blood used until type & screen results are available.
  • Replace calcium — massive transfusion causes citrate toxicity/hypocalcemia.

⚠ Exam Trap — Permissive Hypotension

Goal MAP 50–60 mmHg to avoid disrupting clot formation. Do NOT apply this to a patient with traumatic brain injury — the brain needs adequate cerebral perfusion pressure, so a low MAP is dangerous here.

The Lethal Triad

🌀 Hypothermia → Coagulopathy → Metabolic (lactic) Acidosis

Each one worsens the other two. This is why keeping trauma patients warm is a nursing priority, not just comfort care.

Damage Control Surgery

  • Step 1: Stop hemorrhage, clear contamination, temporary closure (may leave an open abdomen).
  • Step 2: Manage shock in the ICU (warm, correct coagulopathy/acidosis).
  • Step 3: Definitive surgery once the patient is stabilized.

Trauma Complications

  • Airway compromise
  • Hemorrhage
  • Compartment syndrome
  • Rhabdomyolysis
  • Sepsis
  • DIC
  • Acute kidney injury

Compartment Syndrome & Rhabdomyolysis

⚠ Exam Trap — The 5 P's Are LATE Signs

Pulseless, Painful, Paresthesia (numbness), Paralysis, Pallor. By the time you see these, damage may already be occurring — the earliest and most sensitive sign is pain out of proportion to injury, especially pain with passive stretch.

Do frequent neurovascular checks — don't wait for all 5 P's before escalating. Treatment = fasciotomy (incision through the fascia to relieve pressure).

Rhabdomyolysis

  • Muscle tissue breakdown releases myoglobin into the blood (common after prolonged immobility/falls or crush trauma).
  • Myoglobin is a large molecule that clogs the renal tubules → can cause AKI.
  • Classic presentation: weakness, muscle pain, and dark (tea/cola-colored) urine.
  • Treatment: aggressive IV fluid boluses to flush the kidneys.

✔ Summary — Trauma Must-Knows

  • Golden hour = first 60 minutes; right patient, right facility, right intervention.
  • ABCDE primary survey always comes first — return to it if the patient destabilizes.
  • Permissive hypotension (MAP 50–60) is fine for most trauma but AVOIDED in TBI.
  • Lethal triad: hypothermia → coagulopathy → acidosis, each worsening the others.
  • Compartment syndrome 5 P's are LATE findings — pain out of proportion is your earliest clue.
  • Rhabdomyolysis = dark urine + myoglobin clogging kidneys → treat with IV fluids.
Section 2

Spinal Cord Injury

Primary vs. secondary injury, level-of-injury function loss, and telling spinal shock apart from neurogenic shock and autonomic dysreflexia.

12 flashcards · 5 jeopardy Qs

💛 Clinical Pearl — Readmission & Mortality

30% of SCI patients are readmitted within the first year — most often for urinary or skin causes. The most common cause of death in someone living with SCI is pneumonia, followed by sepsis — respiratory and skin care are huge long-term nursing priorities.

Primary vs. Secondary Injury

Primary Injury

The initial mechanical damage — concussion, contusion, compression, tearing, laceration, transection, or ischemia → neuron axonal injury, damage, and necrosis.

Secondary Injury

The body's own inflammatory response makes things worse — swelling/bleeding fill the spinal cavity, compressing blood flow and causing anoxia; free radicals and excess glutamate (excitotoxicity) further damage neurons → cell death.

⚠ Exam Trap — Why This Distinction Matters

Secondary injury is what a lot of acute SCI management is trying to PREVENT (swelling, hypotension, hypoxia all worsen it) — this is why maintaining hemodynamics and oxygenation is a nursing priority, not just comfort care.

⚠ C4 Is the Magic Number for Breathing

Injuries at or above C4 threaten spontaneous respiratory function (phrenic nerve = C3-C4-C5 — "C3, 4, 5 keep the diaphragm alive"). A cervical injury at C1–C4 is a respiratory EMERGENCY — expect to prepare for intubation/mechanical ventilation.

Diagnosis & Medical Management
  • FIRST priority if SCI is suspected: immobilize with a cervical collar and backboard BEFORE anything else.
  • Neuro exam, X-ray of vertebrae, CT/MRI to determine location and extent of damage.
  • Airway management — intubate if necessary.
  • Maintain hemodynamics (neurogenic shock, loss of autoregulation): IV fluid, vasopressors, inotropes to support fluid resuscitation, tone, and cardiac output; pacing to control irregular heartbeat.
  • Patients on vasoactive meds need monitoring — these drugs can decrease perfusion to the GI tract, kidneys, and extremities.
  • Surgical management (decompression/fusion) if: cord compression, progressive deficits, compound vertebral fractures, penetrating injury, or fragments in the spinal canal.

⚠ Exam Trap — Spinal Shock vs. Neurogenic Shock

Both cause hypotension, so students confuse them. The distinguishing feature: spinal shock = temporary loss of reflexes (areflexia) below the injury, while neurogenic shock = loss of sympathetic tone causing the classic triad of hypotension + bradycardia + poor temperature regulation.

They can occur together, but if a question emphasizes bradycardia + temperature dysregulation, think neurogenic shock specifically.

Autonomic Dysreflexia

⚠ Exam Trap — Autonomic Dysreflexia

  • Occurs in 48–70% of patients with injury AT OR ABOVE T6, most often after the first year of injury.
  • A strong sensory stimulus below the injury (classically a full bladder or full bowel) triggers an uncontrolled sympathetic surge.
  • Presentation: severe hypertension (hypertensive urgency/emergency) with compensatory bradycardia (this bradycardia is NOT effective at lowering the BP).
  • Untreated, hypertension can progress to seizure, pulmonary edema, MI, or cerebral hemorrhage.
  • #1 nursing action: find and remove the stimulus first (check for a kinked catheter/full bladder or impaction) before reaching for antihypertensives.
  • Monitor for rapid BP rise (20–40 mmHg above baseline), keep HOB at 45°, check bladder/bowel.

SCI Nursing Priorities

CategoryNursing Focus
AssessmentNeuro, respiratory, urinary, GI; VS (temp ↑/↓, RR ↓, HR ↓, BP ↑/↓); continuous monitoring; strict I&O; skin/surgical sites; pain/anxiety
ActionsMaintain airway/O2, prep for intubation; maintain immobilization; suction equipment ready; cough & deep breathe; manage elimination; positioning/ROM; surgical site care; pain/anxiety management; emotional support
TeachingS/S of respiratory distress; S/S of autonomic dysreflexia; skin care

Recovery Expectations

  • Spinal cord damage itself is NOT reversible — care shifts to lifestyle adaptation and supportive care (ventilation, feeding, mobility, skin care).
  • If the injury is incomplete, some recovery may occur between 6 months and 2 years — this varies significantly person to person.
  • Typical rehab course: 12–18 months (PT, OT, nursing, neurology, psychiatry, etc.).

✔ Summary — SCI Must-Knows

  • C1–C4 injuries threaten spontaneous breathing (phrenic nerve C3-4-5).
  • Complete = no function below injury; incomplete = some function retained.
  • Spinal shock = temporary areflexia; neurogenic shock = loss of sympathetic tone (hypotension + bradycardia + temp dysregulation).
  • Autonomic dysreflexia = injury at/above T6 + full bladder/bowel → severe HTN + reflex bradycardia → REMOVE THE STIMULUS FIRST.
  • #1 cause of death in SCI patients: pneumonia, then sepsis.

Burns

Depth, TBSA, the Parkland-style fluid formula, and the systemic complications that show up once a burn is more than skin deep.

12 flashcards · 5 jeopardy Qs

Burn Etiology

TypeMechanismKey Notes
ThermalFlash (flame), scald (hot water/steam), hot objectMost common type
ElectricalCurrent travels through and burns INSIDE the bodyOften occupational; often with other trauma (falls); can cause organ damage and cardiac/neuro conduction disruption

⚠ Electrical Burns Look Small But Aren't

Electrical burns can look minor on the skin surface but cause deep, unseen damage as current travels through the body — always get an ECG and cardiac enzymes on these patients, and use the LARGER pediatric fluid multiplier (4 mL) even in adults per this course's protocol for electrical burns.

Burn Depth
DepthTissue InvolvedWound AppearancePainHealing
SuperficialMinimal epidermis damageDry, no blisters; pink/red; blanches easilyHypersensitive3–7 days, no scarring
Superficial partial-thicknessEntire epidermis + minimal dermisBlisters (open/closed), weeping; pink/red; mild edema; blanches easilyHypersensitive7–14 days, no scarring

⚠ Full-Thickness Burns Don't Hurt (At the Center)

Students often assume worse burn = more pain. It's the opposite at the center of a full-thickness burn — nerve endings are destroyed, so there's NO pain there (though the surrounding partial-thickness zone is hypersensitive). Absence of pain in a burn wound is a BAD sign, not a good one.

TBSA

Rule of Nines (adult): Head 9%, each arm 9%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, each leg 18%, perineum 1%.

Lund and Browder chart: more precise, adjusts percentages by age (used especially in children, whose head is proportionally larger).

Severity Factors

  • Inhalation injury
  • Location: circumferential burns, hands/feet/genitalia/perineum/joints affect function
  • Patient age, comorbidities, other injuries
Systemic Changes (≥20% TBSA)

💛 Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

  • CO binds to hemoglobin with 200x greater affinity than oxygen — it displaces O2 from Hgb.
  • Pulse ox is NOT accurate in CO poisoning (reads falsely normal/high — can't distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin). Need a carboxyhemoglobin level.
  • S/S progress from mild headache → coma, seizures, death.
  • Treatment: remove from environment, give 100% oxygen, intubation if needed, hyperbaric oxygen.
  • The half-life of CO is 4–8x SHORTER on 100% oxygen — this is why high-flow O2 is given immediately, even before carboxyhemoglobin results are back.

Diagnostic Values to Know

  • Oxygenation: SpO2 (unreliable in CO poisoning), ABG, carboxyhemoglobin
  • Tissue damage: hyperkalemia (cell lysis releases K+)
  • Fluid shifts: decreased BP/CO/venous return; decreased urine output; hyponatremia (Na+ shifts into cells to replace lost K+)
  • Metabolic acidosis: from shock and HCO3- loss along with Na+
  • Dysrhythmias: especially with electrical burns (disrupted cardiac conduction)
  • GI: decreased nutrient absorption/motility but INCREASED nutrient need — metabolic rate can double and fluctuates for up to 3 years post-burn; needs ongoing dietician involvement.
Fluid Resuscitation — The Parkland-Style Formula

💛 Memorize This Cold

  • Formula: mL/kg/%TBSA of Lactated Ringer's
  • Multiplier: 2 mL for adults; 4 mL for children up to age 14 AND for ALL electrical burns (any age)
  • Give the FIRST HALF over the first 8 hours FROM THE TIME OF INJURY (not from arrival!) and the SECOND HALF over the next 16 hours.
  • Fluids are indicated for burns >20% TBSA and treatment starts at the scene — the RN must find out how much fluid was already given and the exact time of injury.
  • Given during the primary survey → it is a priority!

⚠ "From Time of Injury" — Not Time of Arrival

The 8-hour clock for the first half of fluids starts at the TIME OF INJURY, not when the patient arrives at your unit. If 2 hours already passed in the field/transport, the patient only has 6 hours left of that first infusion period on your unit. This is a very common calculation trap.

⚠ Why the 11-Year-Old AND the Electrical Burn Both Use "4"

Two separate reasons trigger the higher (4 mL) multiplier: age under 14, OR an electrical mechanism — regardless of age. Watch for an ADULT electrical burn question; that patient still gets 4 mL/kg/%TBSA, not 2.

Assessing Adequacy of Fluid Resuscitation

Adequate ResuscitationFluid Overload
Urine output 0.5 mL/kg/hr (1 mL/kg/hr if myoglobinuria present); SBP >100, HR <120; CVP 5–10 mmHg; clear lung sounds, normal pH, soft non-distended abdomen, clear mental statusPulmonary edema, peripheral edema, hypertension, compartment syndrome (including abdominal)

Emergent Phase Treatment (Primary Survey)

  • Airway management + C-spine stabilization if trauma suspected
  • Breathing: 100% non-rebreather, humidified once in hospital
  • Circulation: remove ALL clothing/jewelry (patient WILL swell), elevate extremities, neurovascular checks
  • Do NOT elevate the head/use a pillow for facial or neck burns
  • Disability: ongoing neuro exam
  • Exposure: assess extent of wounds, associated trauma, prevent hypothermia

Complications

  • Burn shock, sepsis, AKI, rhabdomyolysis
  • Compartment syndrome — escharotomy or fasciotomy
  • Hypovolemia, infections
  • Deformity (contractures, scars)

⚠ Exam Trap — Escharotomy vs. Fasciotomy

Escharotomy = incision through the burned/hardened ESCHAR only (used for circumferential burns restricting circulation or chest expansion). Fasciotomy = deeper incision through the FASCIA/muscle compartment (used for true compartment syndrome with deep tissue swelling) — this is the same procedure used for trauma-related compartment syndrome in Section 1.

✔ Summary — Burns Must-Knows

  • Burn depth: superficial → superficial partial → deep partial → full-thickness. Full-thickness = NO pain in center (bad sign, not good).
  • Fluid formula: 2 mL (adult thermal) or 4 mL (peds <14 OR any electrical burn) × kg × %TBSA. First half over 8 hrs FROM TIME OF INJURY, second half over next 16 hrs.
  • CO poisoning: pulse ox is unreliable; treat with 100% O2 (shortens CO half-life 4–8x) ± hyperbaric O2.
  • Systemic capillary leak begins around ≥20% TBSA.
  • Electrical burns: small surface wound, big internal damage — always get ECG/cardiac enzymes.

Compartment Syndrome & Rhabdomyolysis (Trauma + Burns Overlap)

Both trauma and burn patients are at risk for compartment syndrome (5 P's: pulseless, painful, paresthesia, paralysis, pallor — all LATE signs) and rhabdomyolysis (dark urine, muscle breakdown → AKI, treated with IV fluids). Circumferential burns specifically add escharotomy as a treatment option in addition to fasciotomy.

⚠ Exam Trap — Bradycardia Can Mean Two Very Different Things

In neurogenic shock, bradycardia is part of the primary problem (loss of sympathetic tone) and is treated with atropine/pacing. In autonomic dysreflexia, bradycardia is a compensatory (reflex) response to severe hypertension and is NOT treated directly — you treat the underlying stimulus and hypertension instead.

Don't let "bradycardia + SCI" autopilot you into one diagnosis — check the blood pressure direction first (low = neurogenic shock; high = autonomic dysreflexia).

Final NCLEX Pearls

💛 Common Near-Miss / SATA Traps

  • "Select all signs of compartment syndrome" — remember ALL 5 P's, and that they are late findings; pain out of proportion often appears first.
  • "Select all nursing actions for autonomic dysreflexia" — searching for and removing the stimulus (bladder/bowel) comes before/alongside antihypertensive treatment.
  • "Select all appropriate fluids for a burn patient" — Lactated Ringer's is the standard answer; know the multiplier depends on age AND mechanism (electrical), not just age.
  • Watch for systemic vs. localized symptom questions — e.g., CO poisoning symptoms (headache → coma) are systemic and can be missed if you're only assessing the burn wound itself.
